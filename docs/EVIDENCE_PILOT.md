# OpenLabel Evidence Check - Pilot (draft v0.2)
Updated 2026-09-30. Legend: [V] verified from source text retrieved; [?] not verified / to do.

## Rubric (same for every study, any funder, any direction)
1. Standing: retracted, corrected, expression of concern?
2. Money and ties: funder, author affiliations, funder's role, disclosure quality.
3. Design quality: bias risks (RoB 2 domains for randomized trials; other tools for observational work).
4. Human relevance: species, population, dose, outcome (clinical vs biomarker).
5. Body of evidence: consistency and certainty (GRADE-style), judged apart from single studies.
6. Contested: rebuttals, disputes, conflicting reviews.
7. Sources (added per PRINCIPLES.md): at least two independent sources, and the strongest case for each side.
8. Relevance log (added): any study set aside must have a written, visible reason.
Rule: record checks 1-5 BEFORE writing the conclusion direction. "Not disclosed" is never scored as "no conflict".
Card format: What the evidence shows / What it does not show / Strongest case on each side / Principle check.

## Card 2 - Ultra-processed food (UPF) and health
Claim: higher UPF intake is linked to worse health.
Key source: Lane et al., BMJ 2024, umbrella review of meta-analyses.
- Standing [?]: not run.
- Money and ties: [V] review reports no funding; none of the included meta-analyses funded by a UPF company. [?] Author COI statements not retrieved.
- Design [V]: 14 meta-analyses, 45 pooled analyses, 9,888,373 participants; observational only. Exposure mostly from food questionnaires. Excess-significance bias in 9 of 28 testable analyses; small-study effects in 5 of 28; moderate-to-high heterogeneity in 45%.
- Human relevance [V]: human cohorts and cross-sectional studies; UPF defined by the Nova system.
- Shows: 71% of outcomes showed a direct association. Class I credibility: cardiovascular mortality (RR 1.50, GRADE very low), type 2 diabetes dose-response (RR 1.12, GRADE moderate), anxiety and common mental disorders (GRADE low).
- Does not show: that UPF itself causes harm, or which ingredient or process is responsible.
- Contested [?]: criticism of the Nova definition not yet checked.
- Strongest case, each side: side A (association) [V]; side B (critiques of Nova and confounding) NOT YET RESEARCHED [?].
- Principle check: FAILS principle 1 (one review, one side). Not publishable yet.

## Card 3 - Sugar industry and heart disease (historical funding case)
Claim: the 1967 NEJM review (McGandy, Hegsted, Stare) is independent evidence on sugar and heart disease.
Key source: Kearns, Schmidt, Glantz, JAMA Intern Med 2016 (internal industry documents).
- Standing [V]: the Kearns paper carries a correction notice (content of correction [?]). Status of the 1967 review [?].
- Money and ties [V]: Sugar Research Foundation sponsored the review, set its objective, supplied articles and received drafts; funding and role not disclosed. Authors paid $6,500 in total.
- Design [V]: the review applied rules that discounted evidence types that threatened sugar and accepted weaker support for the fat hypothesis.
- Limits [V]: no direct proof the industry wrote or changed the manuscript; influence is circumstantial; document set is narrow.
- Shows: hidden funding and a one-sided evidence standard in one influential review.
- Does not show: what the evidence on sugar and heart disease says today. A modern card is needed.
- Contested [V]: the role of sugar was still described as debated in 2016.
- Principle check: partial. Historical case is well documented, but a modern evidence card on sugar is still needed.

## Card 4 - Organic food and health
Claim: eating organic improves human health.
Key source: Vigar et al., Nutrients 2019, systematic review of 35 studies.
- Standing [?]: not run.
- Money and ties [V]: author affiliations include Integria Healthcare, Oliver Nutrition Pty Ltd and Southern Cross University's Centre for Organics Research. [?] Funding statement not retrieved. Flagged the same way as any industry tie.
- Design [V]: few clinical trials measured direct health outcomes; most measured pesticide exposure or indirect markers; small, short (days to weeks); several did not define organic. Cohort studies likely confounded (organic consumers differ in income, education, diet).
- Human relevance [V]: human studies; urinary pesticide metabolites fall on organic diets (e.g., Bradman 2015, n=40) but the health meaning is unclear.
- Shows: lower pesticide exposure on organic diets; observational signals of benefit.
- Does not show: a proven health benefit; authors say evidence does not allow a definitive statement.
- Contested [V]: a 2012 Stanford review found little evidence of health benefit but lower pesticide exposure.
- Strongest case, each side: side A (benefit) Vigar 2019 [V]; side B (little benefit) Stanford 2012 [V, not yet carded].
- Principle check: partial. Two sides identified; Stanford review still needs its own card.
