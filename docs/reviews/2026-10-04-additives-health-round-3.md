## TBHQ (tertiary butylhydroquinone, E319)
Color: yellow (unresolved). Record id: tbhq-intake.

Claim: TBHQ used as a preservative in food harms health at usual intakes.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list TBHQ, tertiary butylhydroquinone or E319. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: JECFA and EFSA both use an acceptable daily intake of 0.7 mg per kg body weight a day, about 49 mg for a 70 kg adult (our arithmetic). In the US, total antioxidants may not exceed 0.02 percent of a food's fat or oil. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: Regulators set an intake limit and estimate usual intake is below it, but EFSA found toddlers and children could exceed it at maximum permitted levels, and a mouse study found TBHQ made egg allergy reactions worse. We found no human studies. The question is unresolved, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- JECFA allocated an acceptable daily intake of 0 to 0.7 mg per kg a day; national estimates put mean intake at about 1 to 20 percent of it.
- EFSA (2016) found that at maximum permitted levels toddlers and children who eat a lot could exceed the limit, and that under refined scenarios no group did.
- A mouse study summarized by Michigan State University found dietary TBHQ increased allergic sensitization and anaphylaxis severity to egg protein.

Does not show:
- That TBHQ in this product harms you. The allergy finding is in mice, with no dose given on the page read.
- That TBHQ causes food allergy in people. The summary says it shows worsening of existing responses in mice and that human effects are unknown.
- The amount in this product. Labels do not give it.
- Current EU or US review status. We did not check for updates after 2016.

Sources:
- Evaluation of national assessments of intake of tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ) (WHO Food Additives Series 42 (JECFA 51st meeting), 1999). https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v042je26.htm. Read. JECFA allocated an ADI of 0 to 0.7 mg per kg body weight a day. National estimates from six countries put mean intake at roughly 1 to 20 percent of the ADI (for example 0.07 mean and 0.14 at the 90th percentile in the US in 1995). Authored by one named expert; declarations not read. Dated.
- Statement on the refined exposure assessment of tertiary-butyl hydroquinone (E 319) (EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (via Japan Food Safety Commission summary), 2016-01-12). https://www.fsc.go.jp/fsciis/foodSafetyMaterial/show/syu04400110149. Read through a Japanese-language summary, not EFSA's own page. EFSA confirmed the 0.7 mg per kg ADI in 2004. At maximum permitted levels, high-consumer toddlers (1 to under 3) and children (3 to 9) could exceed the ADI; under refined scenarios, no group did. Panel declarations not read.
- 21 CFR 172.185: TBHQ (April 2013 edition) (US Code of Federal Regulations (FDA), 2013 edition). https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2013-title21-vol3/pdf/CFR-2013-title21-vol3-sec172-210.pdf. Read. TBHQ may be used as an antioxidant alone or with BHA and BHT, with total antioxidant content not above 0.02 percent of the food's oil or fat. No health data. The 2013 edition was read; current text not checked.
- Preservative tBHQ (Center for Research on Ingredient Safety, Michigan State University, 2025-03-10). https://cris.msu.edu/news/tbhq/. Read. Summarizes a mouse study (Jin, Boss, Bursley, Wilson, Gangur, Rockwell): dietary tBHQ increased IgE, mast cell activation and anaphylaxis severity to egg protein; BHT and 3-hydroxytyrosol did not do this at low concentration; the effect depended on a pathway called Nrf2. The page gives no dose or animal counts, says human effects are unknown and that it shows worsening of existing responses, not causing allergy. The page says the research was in part funded by CRIS; CRIS's own funders were not checked.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. JECFA monograph and CFR text read; EFSA's 2016 statement read only through a Japanese summary and EFSA's 2004 opinion not read. The mouse study was read through a university center summary (partly funded by that center), not the paper. Panel declarations not read. No retraction check run.

## Azodicarbonamide (ADA, E927a)
Color: yellow (unresolved). Record id: azodicarbonamide-safety.

Claim: Azodicarbonamide used in bread dough harms health at usual intakes.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list azodicarbonamide or E927a (dough conditioner in some breads, buns and baked goods). The asthma evidence is about workers inhaling the powder. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: FDA allows up to 45 ppm (0.0045 percent) of the flour weight. FDA estimated adult intake of the breakdown product semicarbazide at about 3 to 56 micrograms a day on average, depending on scenario. JECFA no longer supports any intake limit.

Why this color: The international expert committee withdrew its intake limit in 2023 after a safety concern was raised and no one supplied data for a re-evaluation, the EU does not authorise it, and baking forms breakdown products with animal cancer findings. FDA still permits it at 45 ppm and drew no toxicity conclusion in the document we read. We found no human studies of eating it. The question is unresolved, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- JECFA withdrew its intake limit for azodicarbonamide in 2023 because a safety concern had been raised and no data were offered for re-evaluation.
- The EU says azodicarbonamide is not authorised as a food additive there, that some countries stopped using it, and that semicarbazide can form in treated flour.
- A WHO assessment concluded azodicarbonamide can cause occupational asthma and skin sensitization in workers; FDA permits up to 45 ppm in flour and estimates semicarbazide intake from bread.

Does not show:
- That azodicarbonamide in this product harms you. The asthma finding is about workers breathing the powder, and the cancer findings are for breakdown products in animals at doses we did not compare to bread.
- What the 'safety concern' JECFA mentions was. The database entry does not say.
- Anything about the amount in this product. The label does not say, and baking changes it.
- FDA's current position. The FDA document read draws no toxicity conclusion and we did not read FDA's review list entry.

Sources:
- Azodicarbonamide, JECFA database entry (WHO / FAO Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives, 1965 and 2023). https://apps.who.int/food-additives-contaminants-jecfa-database/Home/Chemical/538. Read. 1965: acceptable treatment level of 0 to 45 mg per kg flour. 2023: ADI withdrawn, noting that a safety concern had been raised and no one supported providing data for a re-evaluation. No doses or counts given; the safety concern is not described on the page.
- Exposure estimate for semicarbazide from the use of azodicarbonamide in bread for the US population (US Food and Drug Administration, undated (label surveys in 2013)). https://www.fda.gov/media/97444/download. Read. Cites 21 CFR 172.806 limits of 45 ppm of flour (0.0045 percent) and says semicarbazide formation 'has raised health concerns'. Estimates adult mean intake of semicarbazide of 3 to 56 micrograms a day depending on scenario (90th percentile up to 99). Draws no toxicity conclusion on the page read. Authors are FDA staff.
- Azodicarbonamide, Concise International Chemical Assessment Document 16 (WHO / IPCS (UNEP, ILO), 1999). https://inchem.org/documents/cicads/cicads/cicad16.htm. Read (summary). Concludes azodicarbonamide can cause occupational asthma and skin sensitization from inhaled dust and that the triggering concentrations are unknown. This is about workers handling the powder, not eating bread; included because it is the main hazard review.
- Alignment of the food additive provisions of commodity standards (European Union comments, Codex Committee on Food Additives, 51st session) (European Union, 2019-03). https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2019-03/codex_ccfa_51_agenda_item-4b.pdf. Read. States azodicarbonamide is not authorised as a food additive in the EU, that a number of countries have stopped using it over safety concerns, that semicarbazide can form in food made with treated flour, and asks JECFA to re-evaluate it as a high priority. A position paper, not a risk assessment.
- Azodicarbonamide (ADA) (Center for Science in the Public Interest (advocacy group), updated 2026-09-18). https://www.cspi.org/chemical-cuisine/azodicarbonamide-ada. Read. Says ADA breaks down in baking to semicarbazide and ethyl carbamate; cites IARC Group 3 for semicarbazide (1987), IARC Group 2A and an NTP 2021 'reasonably anticipated' finding for ethyl carbamate, and that FDA lists ADA among chemicals under review. Advocacy source; the IARC and NTP statements were not checked at their own pages.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. JECFA entry, FDA exposure document, WHO assessment and EU paper read. The cancer classifications for semicarbazide and ethyl carbamate come from an advocacy group's page and were not checked at IARC or NTP. The JECFA safety concern was not described. Not read: the full 21 CFR 172.806 text, FDA's review list entry, any human or bread-specific study. No retraction check run.

## Sorbic acid and sorbates (E200 to E203)
Color: grey (unsupported). Record id: sorbates-intake.

Claim: Sorbic acid and potassium sorbate used as preservatives harm health at usual intakes.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list sorbic acid, potassium sorbate, calcium sorbate, sodium sorbate or E200 to E203. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: EFSA's group acceptable daily intake for sorbic acid and potassium sorbate is 11 mg per kg body weight a day (2019), about 770 mg for a 70 kg adult (our arithmetic); it was 3 in 2015 and JECFA's older figure is 25. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: Regulators have set intake limits and say usual intake is below them, but the limits moved from 25 to 3 to 11 mg per kg as new data arrived, and some cell-culture studies of sorbates found DNA damage markers. We found no human studies of health outcomes. No evidence finding is not a finding of safety, and it is not a finding of harm. No color is shown.

Shows:
- JECFA (1973) set a group acceptable daily intake of 0 to 25 mg per kg a day.
- EFSA (2015) found no genotoxicity for sorbic acid or potassium sorbate and no rat toxicity up to 9,200 mg per kg a day, but set a temporary limit of 3 mg per kg from a reproduction study; its 2019 follow-up raised this to 11 and found exposure well below it.
- A cell-culture study found sodium sorbate raised chromosome damage markers in human blood cells at 100 to 800 micrograms per ml.

Does not show:
- That sorbates are safe, or that they are harmful. We found no human studies on health outcomes.
- That the cell-culture finding applies to eating potassium sorbate. It tested sodium sorbate in cultured cells.
- The amount in this product. Labels do not give it.
- The 2019 EFSA opinion itself, which we read only through the EU regulation that cites it.

Sources:
- Sorbic acid, JECFA database entry (WHO / FAO Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives, 1973). https://apps.who.int/food-additives-contaminants-jecfa-database/Home/Chemical/2443. Read. Group ADI of 0 to 25 mg per kg a day for sorbic acid and its calcium, potassium and sodium salts. Evaluation is from 1973; no reasoning on the page.
- Scientific opinion on the re-evaluation of sorbic acid (E 200), potassium sorbate (E 202) and calcium sorbate (E 203) as food additives (EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food, 2015). https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122436/records/675975f6c7a957febdf784c4. Read (abstract record). No genotoxicity for sorbic acid or potassium sorbate; no adverse effects in rats up to 9,200 mg per kg a day; calcium sorbate excluded from the group ADI for lack of data, with positive genotoxicity for sodium sorbate; temporary group ADI of 3 mg per kg a day (from 300 mg per kg in a rat reproduction study), replacing 25.
- Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/2597 on sorbic acid and potassium sorbate (European Commission, 2024-10-04). https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402597. Read. Records that after new reproductive toxicity data, EFSA's 2019 follow-up set a group ADI of 11 mg per kg a day for sorbic acid and potassium sorbate, with exposure well below it for all groups. The regulation itself is about a new gelatine dessert use.
- Mamur et al., Genotoxicity of food preservative sodium sorbate in human lymphocytes in vitro (Cytotechnology, 2012). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3432536/. Read. Sodium sorbate at 100 to 800 micrograms per ml raised chromosome damage markers in cultured human blood cells. Cell-culture study of sodium sorbate, not potassium sorbate or eating; no people studied. Funded by a university fund; no conflicts declared.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. JECFA entry, EFSA 2015 abstract record, EU regulation and one cell study read. EFSA's 2019 follow-up opinion was read only through the EU regulation. Panel declarations not read. A 2025 systematic review of preservative cell studies mostly concerns benzoate and was not used. No retraction check run.

## Artificial flavors
Color: grey (unsupported). Record id: artificial-flavors-health.

Claim: Artificial flavors, as a listed ingredient, affect health for better or worse.

Applies to: People who put flavors on their watch list, for products that list artificial flavor, artificial flavors or artificial flavoring. Does not cover any single flavor chemical. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: Not stated on labels. Artificial flavors are listed as one ingredient without their components or amounts.

Why this color: Artificial flavor covers many different chemicals that the label does not itemize, and we found no studies of artificial flavors as a group. FDA has removed individual flavoring chemicals when animal cancer data were presented, which shows that individual chemicals can matter but says nothing about this product. No evidence finding is not a finding of safety, and it is not a finding of harm. No color is shown.

Shows:
- US rules define artificial flavor as flavoring that does not come from the plant and animal sources listed for natural flavor.
- In 2018 FDA removed six synthetic flavoring substances (benzophenone, ethyl acrylate, methyl eugenol, myrcene, pulegone and pyridine) from its allowed list under the Delaney Clause, after petitioners' data showed cancer in laboratory animals, and a seventh because industry no longer used it.
- In the EU, about 2,000 flavouring substances have been assessed in groups by EFSA.

Does not show:
- What is in the artificial flavor in this product. The label does not say.
- That any of the removed substances is in this product. FDA's exposure assessment for those chemicals did not indicate a public health risk and companies were given 24 months to reformulate.
- That artificial flavors are safe, or that they are harmful. We found no trials or cohort studies of artificial flavors as a class.
- That natural flavors are better. Consumer Reports' scientist says they are not nutritionally different.

Sources:
- 21 CFR 101.22: Foods; labeling of spices, flavorings, colorings and chemical preservatives (US Code of Federal Regulations (eCFR / GovInfo 2025 edition), 2025). https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title21-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title21-vol2-sec101-22.pdf. Read, paragraph (a)(3) only. Defines natural flavor as an essential oil, oleoresin, essence or extractive, protein hydrolysate, distillate, or any product of roasting, heating or enzymolysis containing flavoring constituents derived from a spice, fruit, vegetable, edible yeast, herb, bark, bud, root, leaf or similar plant material, meat, seafood, poultry, eggs, dairy products or fermentation products of these, whose significant function in food is flavoring rather than nutritional. The labeling paragraphs on how flavors may be declared were not retrieved verbatim.
- FDA removes 7 synthetic flavoring substances from food additives list (US Food and Drug Administration, 2018-10-05). https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-removes-7-synthetic-flavoring-substances-food-additives-list. Read. FDA removed benzophenone, ethyl acrylate, methyl eugenol, myrcene, pulegone, pyridine and styrene from its list of allowed synthetic flavoring substances; six under the Delaney Clause after petitioners' data showed cancer in laboratory animals, one because industry no longer used it. FDA said its exposure assessment did not indicate a public health risk and gave companies 24 months to reformulate. Petitioners were advocacy groups and a scientist.
- Are 'natural flavors' healthier than artificial flavors? (Consumer Reports, 2022-05-10). https://www.consumerreports.org/health/food-additives/are-natural-flavors-healthier-than-artificial-flavors-a6393690728/. Read. Quotes Consumer Reports senior scientist Michael Hansen: no real nutritional difference between natural and artificial flavors, and both are probably safe for most people. Says the specific ingredients generally are not reviewed by FDA, companies are usually not required to list what is in a flavor, and people with food allergies should be careful. Secondary source; funding not applicable (Consumer Reports takes no advertising).
- Flavourings (European Food Safety Authority, 2026). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/flavourings. Read. EFSA advises the European Commission on flavourings. Chemically defined flavouring substances are assessed in groups (about 2,000 substances through Flavouring Group Evaluations). The page names flavouring preparations such as orange oil and vanilla extract as a type of flavouring but does not say whether each is assessed individually.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. Federal regulation, FDA announcement, Consumer Reports article and EFSA topic page read. Petitioners in the FDA case were advocacy groups. No studies of artificial flavors as a class were found. Not read: the 2018 Federal Register rule in full, flavor industry safety program documents, FDA's proposed GRAS rule beyond the natural-flavors record. No retraction check run.
