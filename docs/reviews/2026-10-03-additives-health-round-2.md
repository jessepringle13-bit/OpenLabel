## Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, E320)
Color: yellow (unresolved). Record id: bha-cancer.

Claim: BHA used as a preservative in food can cause cancer when eaten.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list BHA, butylated hydroxyanisole or E320. The cancer evidence is from animals. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: EFSA's acceptable daily intake is 1.0 mg per kg body weight a day (the earlier JECFA figure was 0.5), about 70 mg for a 70 kg adult (our arithmetic). EFSA's intake estimates at current use were generally below it. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: Rats and hamsters fed high doses developed stomach tumors, and IARC classed BHA as possibly carcinogenic. Regulators set intake limits and estimate typical intake is below them. There are no human trials, and one cohort found no increase in stomach cancer. Whether risk exists at food-level intakes is unresolved, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- IARC (Group 2B) found sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in animals and no human data.
- EFSA (2011) found no genotoxicity concern, set an intake limit of 1.0 mg per kg a day and estimated typical intake is generally below it.
- A 2021 review reports forestomach tumors only at high doses and a stomach cancer rate ratio of 0.57 (0.25 to 1.30) for highest versus lowest BHA intake in a Netherlands cohort.

Does not show:
- That BHA at the amount in this product harms you. The tumors are in the rodent forestomach, an organ humans do not have in the same form, and appeared at high doses.
- Whether BHA raises cancer risk in people. The human data are limited to one cohort with a wide confidence interval.
- Current US regulatory action. We did not read FDA's position.
- Anything about BHA in cosmetics.

Sources:
- Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), IARC Monographs Volume 40 and Supplement 7 (International Agency for Research on Cancer, 1986, reclassified 1987). https://inchem.org/documents/iarc/vol40/butylatedhydroxyanisole.html. Read (summary page). Sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in experimental animals (forestomach tumors in rats and hamsters in four experiments); no human data; overall Group 2B, possibly carcinogenic to humans. Not mutagenic in the tests listed. Working Group declarations not read. A hazard classification, not a risk estimate; the evaluation is from the 1980s.
- Scientific opinion on the re-evaluation of butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, E 320) as a food additive (EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food, 2011). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/fr/efsajournal/pub/2392. Read (abstract page). No genotoxicity concern; ADI 1.0 mg per kg body weight a day, from a no-effect dose of 100 mg per kg (growth retardation, deaths and behavior effects in rat pups) with a safety factor of 100, which also covers forestomach changes in rats. Earlier ADIs were 0.5 (JECFA and the EU Scientific Committee for Food, the latter temporary). Refined intake estimates at current use were generally below the ADI. No new dossier was supplied to the Panel. Panel declarations not read.
- Felter et al., Butylated hydroxyanisole: carcinogenic food additive to be avoided or harmless antioxidant important to protect food supply? (Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology, 2021). https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273230021000271. Read (abstract page). Reviews animal and exposure data and reports a stomach cancer rate ratio of 0.57 (0.25 to 1.30) for highest versus lowest BHA intake in a Netherlands cohort, and no increase at typical dietary intake. Rodent forestomach tumors appeared at high doses (for example 10 of 50 rats at 428 mg per kg a day versus 0 of 50 at zero). Funding and author interests were not on the page read; the authors work in toxicology consulting, so independence should be checked.

The 2021 review's funding and interests were not on the page and its authors work in toxicology consulting. Not read: the full EFSA opinion, FDA status, the Netherlands cohort paper, the NTP report. No retraction check run.

## Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, E321)
Color: yellow (unresolved). Record id: bht-intake.

Claim: BHT used as a preservative in food harms health at usual intakes.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list BHT, butylated hydroxytoluene or E321. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: EFSA's acceptable daily intake is 0.25 mg per kg body weight a day, about 17.5 mg for a 70 kg adult (our arithmetic). EFSA found adult exposure unlikely to exceed it; for children it was exceeded at the 95th percentile in Finland and the Netherlands. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: EFSA found no genotoxicity concern and cut the intake limit after newer rat studies, and estimated some children's high-end intake exceeds the new limit in two countries. We found no human studies on health outcomes. The question is unresolved, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- EFSA (2012) set an acceptable daily intake of 0.25 mg per kg a day based on rat reproduction studies, lower than JECFA's earlier 0.3 and higher than the EU committee's earlier 0.05.
- EFSA concluded that BHT is not of concern for genotoxicity and that any carcinogenicity would have a threshold.
- EFSA estimated that 95th percentile exposure of children exceeded the limit in Finland and the Netherlands.

Does not show:
- That BHT in this product harms you. The exceedance is a modeled high-end scenario for children in two countries.
- Anything about human health outcomes. We found no human studies in this review.
- Current US regulatory status or intake. We did not read FDA's position.
- Packaging uses of BHT.

Sources:

Read (abstract page). No genotoxicity concern; any carcinogenicity would have a threshold. New ADI 0.25 mg per kg body weight a day from a no-effect dose of 25 mg per kg in two rat studies; earlier ADIs were 0.05 (EU Scientific Committee for Food) and 0.3 (JECFA). Adult exposure unlikely to exceed it; for children, the 95th percentile exceeded it in Finland and the Netherlands. Panel declarations not read.

(Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology, 2021). https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273230021000271. Read (abstract page). Reviews animal and exposure data and reports a stomach cancer rate ratio of 0.57 (0.25 to 1.30) for highest versus lowest BHA intake in a Netherlands cohort, and no increase at typical dietary intake. Rodent forestomach tumors appeared at high doses (for example 10 of 50 rats at 428 mg per kg a day versus 0 of 50 at zero). Funding and author interests were not on the page read; the authors work in toxicology consulting, so independence should be checked.

## Sulfites (sulfur dioxide, bisulfites, metabisulfites; E220 to E228)
Color: orange (converging). Record id: sulfites-sensitivity.

Claim: Sulfites can trigger reactions in sensitive people, especially some people with asthma.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list sulfites, sulfur dioxide, bisulfite, metabisulfite or E220 to E228. The effect is reported in a minority who are sensitive; most people have no reaction. This app does not know whether you are sensitive. Not medical advice.

Amount: In the US, sulfite at above 10 parts per million in a food or drink must be declared on the label (per a 2012 review). The review reports sensitivity in about 3 to 10 percent of people with asthma. EFSA could not set a safe daily intake and found high consumers below its margin of safety.

Why this color: A long-standing finding, summarized in a review and reflected in US labeling rules, is that sulfites trigger breathing, skin or stomach reactions in a minority of people, most often some with asthma. EFSA also found its safety margin too small for high consumers and could not set a safe intake. This is a reason for sensitive people to look for it, not a finding about people in general.

Shows:
- A review reports that reactions to sulfites include breathing, skin and gut symptoms, most often in people with asthma, with 3 to 10 percent of people with asthma sensitive and 1 to 5 percent of patch-tested people showing skin sensitivity.
- Since 1986 the US has required sulfite above 10 ppm to be declared on labels where its use is allowed.
- EFSA (2022) could not set a safe daily intake, found margins of exposure for high consumers below its level of concern in all groups but adolescents, and recommended more work on hypersensitivity.

Does not show:
- That you are sensitive. Most people tolerate sulfites.
- The amount of sulfite in this product. The label lists the ingredient, not the concentration, and the ppm threshold is a labeling rule.
- Whether the nervous system effects EFSA mentions matter at food intakes. EFSA did not state a dose.
- Anything about sulfites naturally present in wine or dried fruit beyond what the label lists.

Sources:
- Vally and Misso, Adverse reactions to the sulphite additives (Gastroenterology and Hepatology From Bed to Bench, 2012). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4017440/. Read. Sulphite sensitivity can cause skin, gut and breathing reactions, most often breathing reactions in people with asthma; reported prevalence among people with asthma is 3 to 10 percent, and 1 to 5 percent of people patch-tested show skin sensitivity. After FDA action in 1986, sulfite above 10 ppm in foods and drinks where its use is permitted must be declared on the label. Reports from 1985 of more than 250 reactions and six deaths in the US. Funding and competing interests not stated.
- Sulfites: safety concern for high consumers, but data lacking (European Food Safety Authority, 2022-11-24). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/sulfites-safety-concern-high-consumers-data-lacking. Read. EFSA could not set an ADI because toxicity data were insufficient (an earlier temporary ADI of 0.7 mg per kg body weight a day, set in 2016, was not retained). Margins of exposure for high consumers were below the level of concern (80) in all groups except adolescents. Reports possible effects on the nervous system and recommends further study of hypersensitivity. Panel declarations not read.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. Review and EFSA news release read; funding and interests not stated in the review and panel declarations not read. The US labeling rule was read through the review, not FDA's own page. Not read: FDA regulation text, controlled challenge studies, the full EFSA opinion. Only two sources. No retraction check run.

## Propylparaben (E216, E217)
Color: yellow (unresolved). Record id: propylparaben-endocrine.

Claim: Propylparaben used as a food preservative disrupts hormones or harms reproduction in people.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list propylparaben, propyl paraben, E216 or E217. The expert opinion found is about cosmetic use. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: FDA lists a maximum of 0.1 percent in food under good manufacturing practice. The EU cosmetic opinion used an oral no-effect dose of 1,000 mg per kg body weight a day in animals. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: Propylparaben is allowed in US food and called safe in an EU cosmetics review, but that review saw some indications of hormone effects and California will ban it in foods from 2027. No human food studies were found. The question is unresolved, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- FDA lists propylparaben as generally recognized as safe in food at up to 0.1 percent.
- The EU consumer safety committee (2021) found it safe as a cosmetic preservative up to 0.14 percent, but noted some indications of endocrine effects that were not enough to call it an endocrine disruptor.
- California's Food Safety Act bans propylparaben in foods from 1 January 2027, though the law-firm summary does not give California's reasons.

Does not show:
- That propylparaben in this product affects your hormones. We found no human food studies.
- That the cosmetic opinion applies to eating. Exposure and metabolism differ.
- The current EU food status. We did not find a primary source for it in this review.
- How common propylparaben is in current US foods.

Sources:

Lists propylparaben as an antimicrobial agent generally recognized as safe for food use, with a stated maximum of 0.1 percent in food under good manufacturing practice. No acceptable daily intake or health data on the page.
- Opinion on propylparaben, SCCS/1623/20 (European Commission Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety, 2021 (published 2022)). https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2022-08/sccs_o_243.pdf. Read (first part). About cosmetics, not food. Concluded propylparaben is safe as a cosmetic preservative up to 0.14 percent (as acid); some indications of endocrine effects but not enough to classify it as an endocrine disruptor; oral no-effect dose 1,000 mg per kg body weight a day for reproductive effects. Included because it is the most recent expert review of the substance; skin and oral exposure differ. Declarations are held on the Commission register and were not read.
- Food and color additives: is the patchwork here? Secondary source; bill text not read.

## Caramel color (E150c, E150d) and 4-methylimidazole
Color: yellow (contested). Record id: caramel-color-4mei.

Claim: 4-methylimidazole in caramel color raises cancer risk at amounts found in foods and drinks.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list caramel color or E150c or E150d. The ingredient list rarely says which caramel class was used; plain caramel (E150a) and caustic sulfite caramel (E150b) are made differently. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: California's cancer-risk threshold for 4-MEI is 29 micrograms a day. EFSA's group intake limit for caramel colours is 300 mg per kg body weight a day, 100 for E150c. Measured 4-MEI in drinks varies widely by product.

Why this color: Mouse studies found lung tumors and California treats 4-methylimidazole as a cancer-causing chemical, while EFSA judged the colours not carcinogenic and found 4-MEI exposure not of concern. They weigh the same animal data differently. No human studies were found. The question is contested, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- In a 106-week US mouse study, lung tumors rose from 9 of 49 to 22 of 49 in males and from 3 of 45 to 14 of 46 in females at the highest dose, which led California to set a no-significant-risk level of 29 micrograms a day.
- EFSA (2011) found caramel colours not genotoxic or carcinogenic and judged the highest 4-MEI exposure not of concern.
- A 2015 risk assessment of 12 soft drinks estimated lifetime excess cancer risks from about 2 in a million to about 2 in 10,000, depending on the drink and average intake.

Does not show:
- That caramel color in this product raises your cancer risk. The estimates assume California's potency and long-term intake.
- Which caramel class is in this product, or how much 4-MEI. The label does not say; levels vary by product and by manufacturer changes since 2012.
- Anything from human studies. We found none.
- That EFSA and California disagree on the science rather than on how to set safe levels.

Strongest case for:
- NTP mouse study: dose-related alveolar and bronchiolar tumors in both sexes, with trend tests P under 0.01 (as summarized by California OEHHA).
- California lists 4-MEI as a carcinogen and set a 29 microgram a day threshold.
- A 2015 assessment (authors include Consumer Reports staff) estimated excess lifetime risks above 1 in 100,000 for some drinks.

Strongest case against:
- EFSA concluded caramel colours are not genotoxic or carcinogenic and that the highest 4-MEI exposure from E150c and E150d was not of concern.
- EFSA's 2012 update found exposure lower than first estimated.
- The tumors are from mice at high doses, and no human cancer studies were found.

Sources:
- EFSA reviews safety of caramel colours (European Food Safety Authority, 2011-03-08). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/ans110308. Read. Group ADI 300 mg per kg body weight a day for E150a to E150d, with 100 mg per kg a day for E150c. Colours not genotoxic or carcinogenic; no reproductive effects. For 4-methylimidazole (4-MEI) in E150c and E150d, the highest exposure was not of concern. Exposure at maximum use levels could exceed the ADIs for high consumers; a 2012 update (https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/121219) found exposure lower than first estimated, with only E150c in toddlers and high-consuming adults possibly over its limit. Panel declarations not read.
- No significant risk level (NSRL) for the Proposition 65 carcinogen 4-methylimidazole (California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, 2011-10). https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/100711_4MEIfindings.pdf. Read. Based on a 106-week National Toxicology Program mouse study (0, 312, 625 or 1,250 ppm in feed): combined lung tumors rose from 9 of 49 to 22 of 49 in males and from 3 of 45 to 14 of 46 in females at the highest dose (trend P under 0.01). California set a no significant risk level of 29 micrograms a day for 4-MEI (1 in 100,000 lifetime cancer risk). Authors not named.
- Smith et al., Caramel color in soft drinks and exposure to 4-methylimidazole: a quantitative risk assessment (PLoS ONE, 2015-02-18). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4333292/. Read (abstract page). Tested 12 beverages; found a wide range of 4-MEI and estimated lifetime excess cancer risks from about 2 in a million (some colas) to about 2 in 10,000 (one malt drink) at average intake, using California's cancer potency. Several authors are affiliated with Consumer Reports. Funding and conflicts not stated on the page read. A risk estimate from animal data, not a human study.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. EFSA news releases and California's assessment read; panel declarations not read. The 2015 paper was read at abstract level; funding and conflicts not stated on the page, and several authors work for Consumer Reports. Not read: the NTP report, IARC's classification of 4-MEI, FDA's position, current 4-MEI levels in drinks after industry reformulation. No retraction check run.

## Monosodium glutamate and other glutamates (E620 to E625)
Color: yellow (contested). Record id: msg-glutamate.

Claim: Added glutamates such as MSG cause symptoms or harm at usual intakes.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list MSG, monosodium glutamate, glutamic acid, glutamate salts or E620 to E625. Does not cover glutamate that occurs naturally in foods, which the label does not list. Not a medical recommendation.

Amount: EFSA's group intake limit is 30 mg per kg body weight a day, about 2.1 g for a 70 kg adult (our arithmetic). FSANZ reports short-lived symptoms in a small subset after 3 g or more taken without food. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: An Australian and New Zealand review found no convincing evidence of serious harm and only short-lived symptoms in a small subset after large doses, while EFSA set a first-ever safe-intake limit and estimated heavy consumers and children can exceed it. Regulators weigh the evidence differently. The question is contested, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- FSANZ (2003) found no convincing evidence MSG causes severe illness or asthma, and short-lived symptoms in a small subset after 3 g or more without food, with placebo responses confounding the challenge studies.
- EFSA (2017) set a group intake limit of 30 mg per kg a day and estimated it can be exceeded by heavy consumers in all groups and by toddlers and children at medium exposure, and that exposure may exceed doses linked to effects such as headache for highly exposed children.
- A 2019 review of 76 preclinical and 40 clinical papers reports organ effects in animals at high doses and mixed human data.

Does not show:
- That the added glutamate in this product causes you symptoms. Reactions are reported in a small subset, mostly at large single doses.
- Whether usual added intakes cause harm. EFSA's exceedance is for modeled scenarios that include naturally occurring glutamate.
- Differences in how the same dose is tolerated with food. FSANZ reports that eating it with food reduces reactions.
- Anything about yeast extract or hydrolyzed protein, which also contain glutamate but are not covered here.

Strongest case for:
- EFSA (2017): first-ever group intake limit, with estimated exposure above it for heavy consumers in all groups and for toddlers and children at medium exposure.
- EFSA: exposure may exceed doses linked to effects such as headache for highly exposed infants, children and adolescents.
- Animal studies at high doses report organ effects (as summarized in a 2019 review).

Strongest case against:
- FSANZ (2003): no convincing evidence of severe illness, and symptoms in sensitive people were generally temporary and not serious.
- JECFA gave glutamates an intake limit of not specified (per FSANZ).
- The 2019 review concludes human evidence is limited and mixed, and animal doses were far above food intakes.

Sources:
- EFSA reviews safety of glutamates added to food (European Food Safety Authority, 2017-07-12). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/170712. Read. Group ADI of 30 mg per kg body weight a day for glutamic acid and its salts (E 620 to E 625), set for the first time in the EU. Exposure to added glutamates may exceed it for heavy consumers in all age groups and for toddlers and children at medium exposure; including natural glutamate, exposure largely exceeds it. Exposure may exceed doses linked to effects such as headache in humans for highly exposed infants, children and adolescents. Panel declarations not read.
- Monosodium glutamate: a safety assessment (Technical Report Series No. 20) (Food Standards Australia New Zealand, 2003-06). https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/consumer/additives/msg/Documents/MSG%20Technical%20Report.pdf. Read (summary). No convincing evidence that MSG causes severe systemic illness, and it is not a significant asthma trigger. Symptoms resembling the reported MSG symptom complex may occur in a small subset after a large dose (3 g or more) taken without food, are short-lived and not serious, and placebo responses confounded the challenge studies. Notes JECFA gave glutamate an ADI of not specified. Authors not named; dated, so it predates EFSA's 2017 limit.
- Zanfirescu et al., A review of the alleged health hazards of monosodium glutamate (Comprehensive Reviews in Food Science and Food Safety, 2019). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6952072/. Read (abstract page). Reviews 76 preclinical and 40 clinical papers. States EFSA's 2017 ADI of 30 mg per kg a day and that intake estimates exceeded it for all groups, and reports animal studies linking high doses to organ toxicity, with limited and mixed human data. Preclinical doses ranged up to 8 g per kg. Funding and conflicts not captured.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. EFSA news release and FSANZ summary read; EFSA panel declarations not read and FSANZ report authors not named. The 2019 review's funding and conflicts were not captured. Not read: the full EFSA opinion, the JECFA monograph, recent clinical challenge trials. No retraction check run.

## Phosphate additives (E338 to E341, E343, E450 to E452)
Color: yellow (unresolved). Record id: phosphate-additives.

Claim: Phosphate additives in processed food harm kidney, bone or heart health at usual intakes.

Applies to: People who put additives on their watch list, for products that list phosphoric acid or phosphate salts or the matching E-numbers. The strongest concern is in people with reduced kidney function, and EFSA's intake limit does not apply to them. This app does not know your kidney function. Not medical advice.

Amount: EFSA's group intake limit is 40 mg per kg body weight a day (about 2.8 g of phosphorus for a 70 kg adult). EFSA says additives supply 6 to 30 percent of average phosphorus intake and that children and some adolescents can exceed the limit. The label does not state the amount.

Why this color: Additive phosphate is absorbed almost completely, EFSA estimates some children and teenagers exceed its new intake limit, and high phosphate is linked to higher death rates in kidney disease. Evidence for harm in healthy people is described as sparse. Whether usual intakes harm healthy people is unresolved, so no direction is claimed.

Shows:
- EFSA (2019) set a group intake limit for phosphates for the first time and estimated it can be exceeded by infants, toddlers and children at average intake and by adolescents with high intake.
- A 2012 review says phosphate additives are almost fully absorbed versus 40 to 60 percent for natural food phosphate, and cites a cohort of 40,538 dialysis patients linking high phosphate to higher mortality.
- A 2013 review says data on the health effect of phosphorus additives in the general population are sparse.

Does not show:
- That the phosphate in this product harms you. The strongest links are in people with chronic kidney disease.
- Cause in the dialysis cohort, which is observational and in a different population.
- How much phosphate additive is in this product. Labels do not give it.
- Anything about phosphates naturally present in food, which the label does not list.

Sources:
- EFSA issues new advice on phosphates (European Food Safety Authority, 2019-06-12). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/190612. Read. First group ADI of 40 mg per kg body weight a day (about 2.8 g of phosphorus a day for a 70 kg adult). Exposure may exceed it for infants, toddlers and children at average intake and for adolescents with high intake; additives supply 6 to 30 percent of average phosphorus intake. The ADI does not apply to people with moderate to severe reduction in kidney function. Recommends numeric limits for supplements. Panel declarations not read.
- Ritz et al., Phosphate additives in food: a health risk (Deutsches Arzteblatt International, 2012). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278747/. Read (abstract page). Says inorganic phosphate additives are almost completely absorbed, versus 40 to 60 percent for natural food phosphate; estimates additive intake rose from under 500 to about 1,000 mg a day since the 1990s; cites a cohort of 40,538 hemodialysis patients linking high phosphate to mortality. The link is in kidney disease; evidence in healthy people is described as sparse. Funding and conflicts not stated on the page read; dated.
- Gutierrez, Sodium and phosphorus-based food additives: persistent but surmountable hurdles in the management of nutrition in chronic kidney disease (Advances in Chronic Kidney Disease, 2013). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3582990/. Read (abstract page). Reviews why additive phosphorus matters in chronic kidney disease and notes data on the effect of phosphorus additives in the general population are sparse. Funding and conflicts not captured.

Source check (partial): Checked 2026-10-03. EFSA news release read; panel declarations not read. Both reviews were read at abstract level; funding and conflicts not stated or captured, and they date from 2012 and 2013. Not read: the full EFSA opinion, the dialysis cohort paper, trials in healthy people. No retraction check run.
