## 3. Argument ledger
- A1 (supports A): linoleic acid converts to arachidonic acid, which makes pro-inflammatory signalling molecules. Source S7.
- A2 (opposes A): little linoleic acid converts to arachidonic acid; higher intake does not raise tissue arachidonic acid. Sources S4, S6 [?].
- A3 (opposes A): randomized trials in healthy adults found no effect on inflammation markers. Sources S1, S5.
- A4 (opposes A/B): pooled cohort data link higher linoleic acid biomarker levels to lower cardiovascular mortality. Source S10.
- A5 (opposes B): as summarized in the S4 commentary, pooled observational analysis found replacing 5% of energy from saturated fat with linoleic acid linked to 9% fewer coronary events and 13% fewer coronary deaths [?]; primary paper not retrieved.
- B1 (supports B): recovered Sydney trial data reported higher death rates on a linoleic-acid-rich diet (all-cause hazard ratio about 1.62, P about 0.05) and non-significant trends in the updated meta-analysis [?]. Source S2. Figures to re-check against the primary paper.
- B2 (supports B): recovered Minnesota data: cholesterol fell but heart disease and overall deaths did not [?]. Source S3.
- B3 (qualifies B): critiques of the Minnesota reanalysis exist (S9, unread). Reported design caveat: trans fat in the control diet (Minnesota) and unmeasured trans fat in the intervention margarine (Sydney) [?].
- B4 (supports concern, mixed): WHO review reported, via S8, omega-6 linked to 9% lower mortality but 31% higher postmenopausal breast cancer risk (six studies), plus links between a higher omega-6 to omega-3 ratio and cognitive decline and ulcerative colitis [?]. Primary unread.

## 4. Challenge
- A1: mechanism only; A2 and A3 test it more directly and did not support it. Result: weakened.
- A3: human randomized trials, but small, short, in healthy adults, biomarker outcomes. Directness caveat: some included inflammation trials tested arachidonic acid supplements rather than seed oil [?]. S1 is funded by an industry-linked committee (see S1). Result: holds for short-term markers in healthy adults, with a funding flag; longer-term effects unknown.
- A4: observational; one analysis part-funded by a company selling products in this category (restricted grant, no stated role). Result: holds provisionally.
- A5: observational; primary unread. Result: unverified.
- B1-B3: measure deaths and heart disease, not inflammation; old trials with design caveats; secondary-prevention populations. Result: unverified in detail; set aside for Claim A, in scope for Claim B.
- B4: only secondary access; outcomes differ from both claims. Result: unverified; flagged as a separate question outside Claims A and B.
Relevance log: B1-B4 are set aside for Claim A because they measure death, heart disease or other outcomes, not inflammation.

## 5. Outcome (provisional)
- Claim A: unsupported by current human trial evidence; not disproven. The main trial review is funded by an industry-linked committee, trials are small and short, and directness is imperfect.
- Claim B: contested. Reanalysed old trials and observational pooled data point in different directions; critiques unread; several key funding statements unchecked.
- Separate open question: omega-6 and breast cancer, cognition and colitis signals (B4) need primary-source review.
- What would change this: independent long-term trials with inflammation and heart outcomes; completion of funding checks on all sources; primary-paper review of items marked [?].

## 7. Evidence review round 1 (2026-10-03, v0.3)
Primary and fuller sources read (links in the register record):
- Su 2017 (Food Funct, DOI 10.1039/c7fo00433h): 30 randomized trials, 1,377 people. CRP SMD 0.09 (-0.05 to 0.24, 16 studies); IL-6 0.11 (-0.07 to 0.29); TNF-alpha -0.01 (-0.19 to 0.17); adiponectin 0.17 (-0.17 to 0.50); MCP-1 0.14 (-0.33 to 0.60). No heterogeneity or publication bias; few studies for some markers. Abstract only; funding and interests not reached. A 2025 narrative review (Lee and Kurniawan, Nutr Rev, two authors, nothing declared) reports a meta-regression suggesting CRP might rise at very large increases in linoleic acid; not confirmed from the Su full text.
- Johnson and Fritsche 2012 (full text): 15 trials; all inflammation markers null. Funding: unrestricted grant from the ILSI North America Technical Committee on Dietary Lipids. Johnson consulted for Monsanto and Bunge in the prior 5 years; Fritsche none. Authors list low power (largest trial 60 completers), missing baseline ALA data in 10 of 15 trials, and false-negative risk. No confidence intervals reported. This resolves the earlier S1 note: this paper reports no ILSI role for Fritsche; the 2014 commentary discrepancy remains unexplained.
- Marklund 2019 (full text): 30 prospective studies, 68,659 people. LA per interquintile range: total CVD HR 0.93 (0.88-0.99), CVD mortality 0.78 (0.70-0.85), ischemic stroke 0.88 (0.79-0.98), CHD 0.94 (0.88-1.00, not significant). Arachidonic acid not significantly linked to total CVD (0.95, 0.90-1.01). Unilever restricted grant to Tufts (no role stated); Wu and Micha report Unilever support; Mozaffarian reports fees from Bunge, DSM, GOED and others. Biomarker, observational.
- Ramsden 2013 (full text): Sydney Diet Heart Study recovered data, 458 men with a recent coronary event, safflower oil in place of saturated fat. All-cause death HR 1.62 (1.00-2.64), CVD death 1.70 (1.03-2.80), CHD death 1.74 (1.04-2.92). Updated pooled CHD death 1.33 (0.99-1.79), CVD death 1.27 (0.98-1.65). Authors name control-group margarine switching, 29 missing baseline diet records, no fatty-acid measurements, no original protocol, and caution against extrapolating to lower intakes or other groups. One author was an original investigator. No competing interests declared.
- Ramsden 2016 (full text): Minnesota Coronary Experiment, 9,423 randomized (about a quarter stayed over a year). Each 30 mg/dL fall in cholesterol linked to 22% higher death risk (HR 1.22, 1.14-1.32) within the cohort, an observational analysis; no mortality benefit in the intervention group; pooled five trials CHD death 1.13 (0.83-1.54), all-cause 1.07 (0.90-1.27). Diet trans-fat content unknown, as authors state. NIAAA intramural support.
- Cochrane (Abdelhamid 2018, WHO-funded): 19 trials, 6,461 people. All-cause death RR 1.00 (0.88-1.12, low certainty); CVD death 1.09 (0.76-1.55, very low); CVD events 0.97 (0.81-1.15, low); MI 0.88 (0.76-1.02, low); total cholesterol -0.33 mmol/L (high certainty). Cochrane total-PUFA review (CD012345): CVD events RR 0.89 (0.79-1.01, moderate).
- Hamley 2017 (sole author, no funding): adequately controlled trials gave major CHD events RR 1.06 (0.86-1.31), CHD death 1.13 (0.91-1.40), total death 1.07 (0.90-1.26); all trials pooled CHD events 0.87 (0.70-1.07). Author-made trial classification noted as a limitation.
- AHA 2017 advisory (Sacks): core trial meta-analysis RR 0.71 (0.61-0.83) for CHD; advisory states about 30% fewer CVD events when saturated fat is replaced with polyunsaturated vegetable oil. Member disclosures include canola, walnut and beef industry grants and Unilever. Sept 2017 correction concerned Wu disclosure only.
- Lankinen 2019 (Am J Clin Nutr): 62 men, 4-week LA-enriched diet; hsCRP, glucose and arachidonic acid responses differed by FADS1 rs174550 genotype (interaction P<0.05). Direction not in the abstract. Nonindustry funders.
Standing: Crossref shows no correction or retraction for Johnson 2012, Su 2017, Marklund 2019, Ramsden 2013/2016 or Hamley 2017. Heart outcomes are a separate claim from inflammation (Claim B) and are not what the record shows.
Not done: the Su 2017 full text (funding), the Ramsden 2016 critiques, the WHO omega-6 observational review, other newer RCTs.
Outcome (unchanged label, provisional): Claim A is not supported by human trial evidence on inflammation markers; not disproven. Claim B remains contested.

## 8. Government sources read: Dietary Guidelines 2025-2030, Scientific Foundation, MAHA report (2026-10-03)
- Dietary Guidelines (HHS and USDA, Jan 2026), full text of fat passages: no mention of seed oils, vegetable oils, linoleic acid or omega-6. Quotes: "prioritize oils with essential fatty acids, such as olive oil. Other options can include butter or beef tallow." "saturated fat consumption should not exceed 10% of total daily calories." "More high-quality research is needed to determine which types of dietary fats best support long-term health." No reference numbers in those passages.
- Scientific Foundation (supplemental evidence review that underpins the guidelines): argues, citing Ramsden 2016 and Appendices 4.6 and 4.7, that RCTs of replacing saturated fat with linoleic-acid-rich oils show no CHD or mortality benefit, with the Oslo trial excluded as confounded; certainty rated moderate for no effect on mortality, very low for CHD events. Says "the concern is not the presence of linoleic acid in the diet but its concentration and source" and that long-term effects of refined-oil exposure are "insufficiently studied." Appendix 2 lists proposed trials; one says linoleic acid-rich oils for cooking are "potentially harmful, but we do know for sure" (sic) and another says the net effect on cardiometabolic and liver outcomes at current intake "remains uncertain" while experimental data suggest linoleic acid influences inflammatory and pain pathways. It does not claim in the passages read that seed oils cause inflammation. Extraction was by passage search; not every page was read.
- How it compares with our record: its conclusion on heart outcomes matches Hamley 2017 and Ramsden 2013/2016 and conflicts with AHA 2017 and the independent Cochrane rating (low certainty). On inflammation it adds no new trial evidence; it calls for one. Our 2017 meta-analysis of 30 trials and the Lankinen genotype signal remain the human evidence on markers.
- Authors and ties (from the report disclosures, 3-year window): Ty Beal (WHO animal-source foods group; FAO livestock committee), Benjamin Bikman (book royalties), J. Thomas Brenna (Global Dairy Platform, GOED, Danone/Nutricia, National Cattlemen's Beef Association/Texas Beef Council consulting and grant, General Mills, American Dairy Science Association, seafood nonprofit board), Michael Goran (infant formula and supplement advisory roles; Atkins Foundation), Donald Layman (Cattlemen's Beef, National Dairy Council), Heather Leidy (General Mills, Cattlemen's Beef, National Pork Board, Novo Nordisk), Ameer Taha (Fonterra, California Dairy Research Foundation, Dairy Management Inc.; patents), Jeff Volek (Virta Health equity; Simply Good Foods advisory), Daisy Zamora (none disclosed; co-author of Ramsden 2016 and credited with Appendix 4.6, with Goran). The report's disclosure form was streamlined and limited to nutrition-related interests. A PubMed record lists Zamora as second author of the 2016 BMJ paper.
- MAHA Commission assessment (White House, May 2025): one passage on seed oils: "industrial fats from refined seed oils ... contribute to an imbalanced omega-6/omega-3 ratio, a topic of ongoing research for its potential role in inflammation." Single citation: Simopoulos 2008 (Exp Biol Med 233:674-688), a review by the author whose book the movement cites; not opened. The report separately argues against "reduce saturated fat" advice (citing Tapsell 2016 and Mozaffarian 2018). It makes no stronger claim than "ongoing research."
- Not checked: the MAHA strategy report (Sept 2025), the Simopoulos 2008 paper, the other Foundation appendices, critiques beyond news summaries.
